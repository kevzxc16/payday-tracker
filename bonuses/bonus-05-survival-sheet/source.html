Pay these first, in this order

  1. Shelter Rent or mortgage. Eviction or foreclosure is catastrophic and hard to undo. If you can't pay the full amount, call your landlord or bank now — script on page two.
  2. Food Basic groceries to get you through. Don't skip meals to save money — it leads to expensive convenience eating later.
  3. Utilities that shut off Heat in winter, AC in summer extremes, water, electricity, phone. Most have hardship programs — ask before they shut off.
  4. Health and safety Prescriptions you actually need. Child care that lets you work. Anything legally required.
  5. Transportation to work Gas, bus pass, or the car payment if you need the car for your job. Losing your job costs more than any single bill.
  6. Insurance Car insurance (legally required), health insurance if you have it. A lapsed policy costs more to restart than to maintain.
  7. Debt minimum payments Minimums only, on each active debt. Missing a minimum adds late fees that compound the problem.
  8. Everything else — defer or cut Streaming subscriptions, gym memberships, takeout, non-essential shopping. These wait until next payday.

The toolkit

when you need to ask

Two things matter most in a hard period: asking for grace before you need it, and not making the problem worse with a fast "solution." Both are below.

Calls to make today

People are more flexible than systems. Asking before you miss a payment almost always goes better than asking after.

Calling your landlord "Hi [name], it's [you]. I want to give you a heads up that I'm going to be short on rent this month by [amount]. I'll have the rest by [date]. Can we work something out?"
Calling a utility company "Hi, I'm calling because my payment will be late this month. I'd like to ask about your hardship program, or set up a payment plan so my service doesn't get interrupted."
Calling a credit card company "Hi, I'm calling to ask if you can lower my minimum payment for this month due to financial hardship. I want to keep making payments, just smaller ones until next paycheck."
Two things to remember

Be brief. Don't apologize ten times. Say what you need, offer a date, ask if it's possible. That's it.

What not to do

The "easy" solutions are the most expensive. Each of these turns a tight month into a tight year.

  • Payday loans or cash-advance apps Effective APRs of 300–600%. A $300 loan often costs $400+ to repay within weeks. They're designed to roll over.
  • Title loans on your car You can lose your car. Then you lose your way to work. Then it's not just a tight month.
  • Credit card cash advances Higher APR than purchases, no grace period, fees from day one. Use the card to buy what you need instead — the math is better.
  • Borrowing from your 401(k) Taxes plus a 10% penalty if you're under 59½. You're paying to undo a year of retirement growth.
  • Buy-now-pay-later for essentials Groceries on installment plans means you'll be paying for this month's food two months from now, on top of that month's food. It compounds.
After this period

One tight period doesn't mean every period has to be tight. On your next reset, set aside even $10 toward an emergency fund — the goal isn't to have a thousand dollars saved tomorrow, it's to have any buffer before the next hard period arrives. Buffers, not budgets, are what break the cycle.
